An administrative workflow feature. Not a clinical decision-support, prescribing or insurance-adjudication tool.

The problem today

Prior-auth work lives in fragments

Faxes on the counter. Notes in someone's head. A spreadsheet. An insurer portal. A phone queue. A folder of PDFs.

Nobody can answer these quickly
Where is this case right now?
The status lives with whoever touched it last. Patients often discover it only by calling the pharmacy, then the clinic, then the insurer — and each call starts the reconstruction again.
What is still missing from it?
Requirements differ by insurer and by plan, so gaps usually surface as a rejection or an “additional information” letter weeks later — and the clock restarts.
Who owns the next step?
The work lands wherever the fax landed. When a case belongs to “the team”, handovers between shifts quietly drop the next step — until the patient calls.
When do we chase the insurer?
Insurers rarely announce delays — public special-authority queues have stretched to weeks10. Without a recorded expected-response date, chasing happens only when someone remembers.
When does the authorization expire?
Nobody sends a warning that the clock has run out. The expiry sits in a filed approval letter, and renewals that need weeks of lead time start at the counter, after a claim rejects.
What exactly did we send, and when?
A fax confirmation page is often the only evidence. When the insurer says “never received”, it is memory against their log — so the package is sent again, and the wait restarts.

The hidden tax: re-discovery

The single biggest cost is not any one step — it is repeatedly re-establishing what a case is, what it needs, and who last touched it. It happens several times per case and never appears on a report.

Key-person risk

Prior-auth knowledge usually sits with one or two experienced staff. When they are off, complex cases stall — and when they leave, the knowledge leaves too.

Lapses discovered at the counter

Authorizations expire quietly. The pharmacy usually finds out when a claim rejects with the patient standing there — turning planned work into an emergency.

Two details we refuse to blur: in Alberta, public-plan special authorization is completed and signed by the prescriber1, and most private prior-authorization forms are physician-completed too4 — AlgoPharma prepares the package, tracks the hand-off and records the outcome; it never submits on anyone’s behalf. Beyond the hours

What else changes — and how honest we are about it

These matter more to a pharmacy owner than the hourly maths. They are also harder to attribute, so we put them here rather than in the headline number.

EffectWhy it mattersOur honest caveat
Fewer avoidable denials An incomplete package means a full rework cycle — hours of work plus weeks of delay for the patient. Depends on your current first-time-complete rate, which most pharmacies do not measure yet.
Fewer lapsed authorizations A lapse means interrupted therapy, an emergency scramble, and an unhappy patient at the counter. Most pharmacies do not know their lapse count until they start tracking it.
Patient retention A patient whose high-cost therapy is interrupted may move to another pharmacy permanently. Attribution is genuinely difficult — do not build the case on this alone.
Capacity to grow Recovered hours absorb more specialty prescriptions without adding headcount. Only converts to revenue if the local demand exists.
Lower key-person risk Prior-auth knowledge stops living with one experienced person. Shows up as resilience, not as a line item.

Our position: build the business case on the staff time — it is measurable and defensible. Treat denial reduction, lapse prevention and retention as upside to be verified. A vendor that puts those in the headline number is guessing.

What we need from you

A deliberately small set of data

An administrative workbench, not a clinical record. Enough to track the case — and nothing more.

What a case needs
De-identified patient reference — your patient ID, aliased
Medication, DIN, dosage form — from the prescription already in your PMS
Insurer, plan, coverage category — from the insurance card
Prescriber office contact — for chasing documents
Case owner — assigned by you
Key dates — submitted, expected response, approval, expiry
Authorization number and the documents received

Set up once: your stores, your team and their roles, your preferred lead times. Coverage rules are maintained and human-reviewed on our side — you consume them, you don't build them.

What we deliberately do not need
No diagnosis, symptoms or clinical notes as data
No lab values, vitals or clinical scores
No patient names in daily working views
No dispensing history, adherence or refill patterns
No connection to your dispensing engine — nothing written back, no claim transmitted

This is deliberate, not a gap. Minimizing identifiable clinical data keeps the feature administrative and makes the privacy conversation materially simpler than for a clinical system — under PIPEDA and the applicable provincial statute (Alberta HIA, Ontario PHIPA, BC PIPA). Your privacy officer and counsel remain responsible for the final assessment.

Starting is light. Begin by entering only your currently open cases — history is not required. PMS integration (Kroll, PharmaClik Rx) is the next milestone, not a maybe: it removes double entry and closes the approval-to-billing loop. Value starts before it — and compounds with it.
Scope and safety

What AlgoPharma does not do

Stated plainly, because it defines the risk profile of adopting it — and because a partner should hear it from us first.

Does not diagnose, interpret symptoms or assess clinical suitability
Does not recommend medications, suggest switching, or advise on dosing
Does not renew prescriptions or contact prescribers automatically
Does not approve insurance, adjudicate claims or guarantee reimbursement
Does not replace your PMS, insurer portals or submission channels
Does not replace pharmacist or physician judgment

Final clinical decisions remain with the prescribing physician and pharmacist. Final reimbursement decisions remain with the insurer.
